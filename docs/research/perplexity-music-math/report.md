Music-integrated mathematics instruction is promising when students use rhythm, duration, grouping, or musical patterns to represent mathematical relationships—and teachers explicitly connect those experiences to diagrams and symbols. The clearest classroom evidence concerns fractions in grades 3–4, with additional encouraging Israeli research on patterns and symmetry in grade 3. Evidence is weaker for general number sense, middle-school ratio learning, and lasting reductions in mathematics anxiety. This is not evidence that background music or ordinary instrumental lessons automatically improve mathematics.

2. Fractions: the strongest direct classroom evidence
Academic Music: grade 3, United States

Courey, Balogh, Siker, and Paik’s 2012 study included 67 third-graders in one multicultural, mixed-socioeconomic school. Students received either regular mathematics instruction or 12 Academic Music sessions: twice weekly, 45 minutes per session, for six weeks. Activities included notation, clapping, drumming, chanting, and explicit connections between musical durations and fractions.

The direct evidence is most relevant to:

Equal parts of a fixed whole.

Relative fraction magnitude and equivalence.

Coordinating musical, visual, and symbolic representations.

Addition and subtraction of fractions within the studied curriculum.

4. Mathematics anxiety and motivation

The evidence here is suggestive but less causally secure than the fractions findings.

A grade-7 Philippine action-research study by Insorio (2025) followed 100 students during two months of mathematics songs and dance. Students reported lower anxiety and higher behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and social engagement. However, there was no concurrent control group; the intervention combined music, movement, social participation, and changes in teaching. It cannot establish that music itself caused the changes.
ijods

A small MuSciQ technology-supported mathematics project with 12 fourth-graders reported reduced mathematics anxiety and increased motivation. Its scale makes it pilot evidence, not a reliable estimate for schools generally.
scholarworks.iupui

Teacher-education and classroom literature describes enjoyable collaboration, participation, and willingness to engage with challenging tasks. Such observations support feasibility and acceptability, but are not interchangeable with validated anxiety outcomes or durable achievement gains.

5. Which approaches and grade levels look best?

“Best supported” is more defensible than “proven best.”

Most supported: grades 3–4, closely matched mathematical structures

The clearest combination is duration-based fraction teaching in grades 3–4, and symbolic musical-pattern work in grade 3. These studies connect a specific musical relationship to a specific mathematical concept and assess mathematics beyond musical performance.
eric.ed
+2

Promising but less established: grades 1–2

Counting, equal grouping, and repeating patterns are plausible instructional uses. A five-week study with 46 first- and third-graders demonstrated classroom implementation, but its small, selected classroom sample and potential novelty effects limit causal conclusions. It does not establish an optimal early-primary method.
files.eric.ed

Insufficient evidence for a middle-school “winner”

The retrieved evidence does not identify a robustly replicated music-integrated approach for grades 6–8 ratio or number learning. One randomized grade-6 study with 120 students used geometric shapes to teach irregular musical meters, but its principal outcome was musical-meter learning—not mathematics achievement. It should not be cited as proof that music teaches geometry or ratios.
files.eric.ed

Features worth prioritizing

Across the more relevant classroom programs, the recurring design is:

Perform, hear, or create a mathematical structure.

Represent it visually.

Name the mathematical relationship explicitly.

Write the conventional symbols.

Solve related mathematics without the musical support.

This pattern is better supported than adding music as decoration. However, the studies do not isolate each component, so it remains uncertain whether sound adds benefit beyond equally well-designed visual/manipulative instruction.

Essential safeguards

Fix and name the whole. A quarter note represents one quarter of the selected whole-note/four-beat unit in these activities—not automatically one quarter of any bar.

Distinguish number of sounds from duration. Two short sounds can occupy the same time as one longer sound.

Preserve visual and symbolic work. The successful fraction programs included fraction strips, circles, number lines, notation, and mathematical explanations.

Assess the intended mathematics, not just musical fluency. A student can reproduce a rhythm without understanding the associated fraction. These safeguards follow directly from the representational structure of the studied lessons.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih

For accessibility and participation, provide a quiet visual/tapping alternative, avoid requiring solo performance, and do not make precise rhythmic execution a prerequisite for demonstrating mathematical understanding. These are implementation precautions rather than experimentally isolated ingredients of the interventions.

7. Limitations and implications for teachers

The principal limitations are small numbers of classrooms, single-school samples, teacher/treatment confounding, curriculum-aligned tests, and multicomponent interventions. Several studies report impressive effects, but impressive effects from a few classrooms are not the same as stable effects across schools. The Israeli fraction study’s three- and six-month follow-ups are valuable, yet replication across many classrooms remains necessary.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih
+2

A sensible classroom evaluation would therefore:

Compare the musical lessons with equally engaging mathematics lessons covering the same content for the same amount of time.

Use multiple classrooms per condition where possible.

Assess immediate learning, delayed retention, and unfamiliar problems without music.

Measure achievement, anxiety, and motivation separately.

If evaluating an app, compare the same tasks and feedback with and without mathematically informative sound.

That design would address the main uncertainty left by the literature: whether musical representation contributes something beyond good explicit instruction, additional representations, and engaging practice.

+2

For a project such as MelodyMath, the most defensible starting point is grades 3–4, fixed-whole durations, fraction equivalence, and patterns—with explicit transitions to silent mathematical tasks. The evidence supports developing and testing that instructional approach; it does not yet justify claiming that a music-based app broadly improves all elementary mathematics or reliably treats mathematics anxiety.
